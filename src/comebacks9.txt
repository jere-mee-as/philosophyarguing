# Second-wave Continental, Theory, Political Thinkers, Politics (thirty).

## Existentialism
> We must create
< The choice isn't unconstrained. It's made in a situation, in front of others, and you answer for it. What's denied is a ready-made answer handed down.
< Pick naps and then live it, in front of everyone, for forty years. The seriousness comes from that part.
< Teenagers ask the question. The French kept asking it past twenty, which is the difference.
> Authenticity
< It was never the whole ethic. It's the condition for having one: owning your choices instead of crediting them to your role, your nature or your orders.
< They were being what their party, their rank and their mob told them to be. That's the opposite.
< That's sincerity, and it's cheap. Authenticity is admitting you chose to be rude.
> Anxiety reveals
< The claim is about a particular mood with no object, different from fear of something. That it sometimes has a chemical trigger doesn't tell you what it shows.
< Coffee gives you jitters about nothing. Now ask why "about nothing" is unbearable.
< Two names because two languages noticed the same thing separately.
> Existence can't be captured
< The big books describe structures every existence has, like being in a situation and facing death. They leave the content to each person, and that was the claim.
< Seven hundred pages on why no page can live it for you. He was thorough.
< Required reading that tells you nobody can do your reading for you.

## Phenomenology
> Go back
< The descriptions get corrected against each other, as observations do. Merleau-Ponty's account of perception displaced Husserl's on specific points.
< They came back with different parts of the same thing and argued about how they fit. That's a field.
< The forty pages show how much you were taking for granted about the cup. That's the rigor.
> Bracket
< Full suspension may be impossible, and he came to say so. Even a partial one shows how much of what we "see" is assumption.
< It's a working assumption that gets tested by trying it. That's allowed.
< Try it for one minute. You'll find out how much you never stopped assuming.
> Consciousness is always
< Moods are about the world as a whole: in boredom everything shows up as flat. They have a wider object.
< A headache is of your head, as hurting. Ask where it is and you'll point.
< And that nobody had said how. That part took the next hundred years.
> It's a rigorous science
< The results exist and went elsewhere: the lived body, the horizon of perception and the structure of time-consciousness are now standard in cognitive science.
< Psychologists and roboticists use the results. Being absorbed by other fields is what success looks like.
< He restarted because he found the earlier version wasn't rigorous enough. That's the standard he was applying.

## Levinas
> Ethics is first
< The claim is that the demand another person makes on me doesn't wait for my theory of what they are. It's checked against every encounter you've had.
< Someone asks you for help before you've worked out your metaphysics. That's the order he means.
< The clarity comes when someone needs something from you. It's in the text less.
> The face
< He's describing where obligation starts. He isn't offering a decision procedure. Weighing claims comes with the third person, and he says that's where justice and institutions begin.
< "Do I know you?" is already an answer to someone. You were addressed before you decided.
< Try refusing someone while looking at them. Then tell me it's nothing.
> My responsibility
< Infinite means it's never discharged, so you can't say "I've done my share" and stop. The guiding is done by justice, which compares and limits.
< Behind is the correct self-assessment. He wrote the philosophy of knowing you are.
< It's why you feel bad about the dish.
> Western philosophy
< It's a claim about a tendency: to understand is to fit a thing into what's already known. He names exceptions, Plato's Good beyond being and Descartes's idea of the infinite.
< He credits Plato and Descartes with noticing first. He says so repeatedly.
< He lost his family to people who were certain they'd understood who the Other was. He's entitled to a theme.

## Cioran
> It would have been better
< He grants there's no one to be better off. The remark is a judgment from inside life on what it costs, and living to 84 was his evidence.
< The empty chair has never had a bad night.
< He said the idea of an exit was what let him stay. The nice street doesn't contradict it.
> Consciousness is a disease
< It's a figure for excess: awareness beyond what living requires, which no other animal carries. The comparison class is everything else alive.
< Every doctor is a symptom reviewing symptoms. It works for them.
< He asked the mystics and the Stoics. They concurred and prescribed more of it.
> Lucidity
< He doesn't prove it and says proof belongs to systems. He reports what's left when the consolations are examined one at a time.
< He was funny about it, which the merely miserable never are.
< Seven years of insomnia and the best French prose of the century. Some people get neither.
> Only the aphorism
< An aphorism can be judged on the spot, with nothing around it to lean on. A system lets a weak step hide behind the strong ones.
< He contradicts himself constantly and says that's the honest record of a mind.
< A good one takes longer than the chapter you'd write instead.

## Dostoevsky
> Without God
< The claim concerns justification. People behave decently on inherited habits, and he's asking what happens over generations once the ground is seen to be gone.
< Believers who did evil broke their own law and knew it. That's a different condition from having none to break.
< He gave it to his most intelligent character and couldn't fully answer him. That's honesty.
> Reason alone
< He gave the other side its strongest speech. Readers still argue over whether the Inquisitor is answered, which a rigged book wouldn't allow.
< He wrote the Inquisitor so well that atheists quote it. An author rigging the game doesn't arm the other side.
< He'd watched the real ones in the 1860s. Some of them did.
> Suffering redeems
< He never says suffering is good. He says a person can refuse or accept the truth it shows them, and Ivan's objection about children stays in the book unanswered.
< He doesn't say it redeems on contact. Raskolnikov suffers for five hundred pages and it does nothing until he stops lying.
< He wrote that down as well. He's the least excused person in his own books.
> The Underground Man
< It hits the premise that people act for their advantage once they see it. If they'll wreck their interest to prove they aren't piano keys, the planned society fails.
< One of the two, and it wrecks every plan built on people doing what's good for them.
< The first blog, and the comments section, in one voice.

## Frankfurt School
> The culture industry
< Audiences reinterpret what they're sold and still buy the next one. The claim is about the form, standardized and pre-digested, and that survives lively fans.
< The sixties were on vinyl by 1969 and in commercials by 1975. He predicted the second part.
< He heard the dance-band jazz of 1930s radio, the product he was writing about.
> Enlightenment reason
< The book says so itself: it's reason criticizing a narrowing of itself into mere calculation, for the sake of the wider kind. Habermas built on that.
< They used one kind of reason against another. There's more than one, and that was the argument.
< Two refugees from the real thing, noticing what it had in common with the radio. They'd earned the comparison.
> Critical theory unites
< The aim is fixed only as less needless suffering. What would bring that about is left open to inquiry, and they revised their own answers repeatedly.
< Medicine knows it wants the patient well before it starts. Nobody calls that bias.
< He thought their tactics would bring a backlash, and he said so in advance. It did.
> Tolerance of the intolerable
< The essay argues that formal neutrality favors whoever already owns the megaphones. Who decides is its weak point, and he admits as much.
< It says tolerance that treats the megaphone and the leaflet alike isn't neutral. That takes more than a sentence.
< He was talking about a few broadcasters owning nearly every channel. That detail usually goes missing.

## Baudrillard
> We live in simulation
< It can be stated because it's a matter of degree. Models now precede the events they describe: polls, forecasts, brands. He names the tipping point.
< He's describing the missing thing from memory, like everyone over forty.
< The money's a number on a screen, backed by confidence in other numbers. He'd take the example.
> Signs now refer
< The rain is real. What it means to you, a ruined event or a trending storm with a name, arrives pre-packaged, and that layer is what he's describing.
< People stop for the sign with no car in sight. They're obeying the sign.
< He says Disneyland is there to make the rest look real by contrast. Noticing it's fake is the first step and he took the second.
> The Gulf War
< The essays came out before, during and after. The thesis is that what the West experienced was a televised event with a fixed outcome, and he says the dead were real.
< He says so. The claim is that for the audience it wasn't a war, it was programming, and for one side it was a slaughter.
< You do have to read it. It's forty pages.
> The Matrix
< Right, and his fans say the same. The film's error is instructive: it imagines a real world you could wake up into.
< The movie made more sense because it kept the door marked "exit."
< The filmmakers opened the book, put the money in it and didn't read it. He couldn't have staged it better.

## Lacan
> The unconscious is structured
< He means it works by substitution and displacement, as jokes and slips do. Freud's own examples are wordplay, and that's the evidence.
< You speak it fluently every time you say the wrong name.
< Several thousand analysts trained in it. It's a crowded language.
> The mirror stage
< The mirror is the example. The claim is that the self is first met from outside, as an image held together by someone else's gaze, and a parent's face will do.
< A mother's face did the job before glass. He says the mirror can be a person.
< From a baby delighted by its reflection six months before it can walk. It's a good observation.
> Desire is the desire
< The readings are meant together: we want what others want, we want to be wanted, and we want from a place others shaped. It's compressed, on purpose.
< You want the sandwich you saw in an ad, at the place your friends go. Somebody else is involved.
< It means three things because desire does.
> The Real
< It names the point where the patient's story breaks down, in trauma or repetition. Clinically, that's where the work is.
< Thirty years circling a thing that can't be said is called an analysis.
< Every theory has a remainder. His is the only one with a chapter on it.

## Jung
> There's a collective
< He agrees it's biological: inherited patterns of response, the way a chick fears a hawk's shadow. "Collective unconscious" is his name for the shared equipment.
< Everybody has a mother, and every culture tells the same six stories about her. That's the thing to be explained.
< He went looking, in patients who'd never read a myth and dreamed one anyway.
> Archetypes
< They're patterns of situation: threat, guide, betrayal, return. Screenwriters and game designers use them because audiences respond on schedule.
< It's casting that works in every country without translation. Ask why.
< Zodiac signs don't predict what scares a three-year-old.
> Synchronicity
< He called it a hypothesis about meaning and said it wasn't causation. Its use is clinical: what a coincidence means to the patient tells you about the patient.
< The nine thousand didn't arrive on the day it mattered. That's what he's pointing at.
< He co-wrote it with a Nobel physicist, who didn't think it was arithmetic.
> Individuation
< The map is his. That midlife turns people from achievement toward meaning is among the better-replicated findings in the psychology of aging.
< He used his own dreams, his patients' and the alchemists', who he argued were describing the same process in other symbols.
< He disowned being typed. Introvert and extravert are his, and they held up.

## Structuralism
> Meaning comes from differences
< The world supplies the things and the system supplies the cuts. Where one language has a single word and another has two, the difference is in the system.
< Cats help, and then English has "cat" and "kitten" where another language cuts elsewhere.
< A dictionary does define every word with other words, and it works.
> The sign is arbitrary
< It's modest and it's the foundation: no sound has a natural meaning, so meaning has to come from the system. Stretching it was other people's doing.
< The label is arbitrary and what counts as "poison" versus "medicine" is a line the language draws. Ask a pharmacist about dose.
< Students assembled the Gospels too.
> Myths and kinship
< The kinship work made testable claims about which marriage systems can exist, and anthropologists argued over the data for decades. That's replication being attempted.
< Two columns and a myth, and then predicting the variants found in the next valley. He did that part.
< Raw and cooked is nature and culture, which every society marks. He picked the kitchen because every society has one.
> The subject is an effect
< A theorist shaped by structures can still describe them, as a native speaker can write a grammar. Being inside a system doesn't stop you mapping it.
< The royalties go where the contract says. That's a structure.
< The name on the cover is a legal function. One of them wrote a famous essay on exactly that.

## Mark Fisher
> It's easier to imagine
< The claim is about what's publicly thinkable: no party, film or mainstream program presents an alternative as live. The bookstores are the niche he describes.
< The second chapter is missing from all of them, and that's his point.
< It's a name for why nobody has one. A diagnosis isn't a prescription.
> Capitalism has absorbed
< He doesn't say resistance is impossible. He says its usual forms were already priced in, and asks what a form that isn't would look like.
< He says exactly that about his own book, in the book.
< It sold to people who felt that and had no word for it. That's what books are for.
> Culture has stalled
< His test is concrete: play a record from 1975 to someone in 1955 and it would shock them. Play one from 2015 in 1995 and it wouldn't.
< He was listening to new music weekly and writing about it. The claim came from someone keeping up.
< Sad that a future was promised, budgeted for and cancelled. That's a political fact.
> Mental distress
< He doesn't say every case. He says treating a mass rise in distress as millions of private chemical accidents is itself a political choice.
< Job's troubles came from on high, were nobody's fault he could find, and he was told to look within. Good example.
< The mugs are how you know the claim got absorbed. He covered that.

## Accelerationism
> Speed capitalism up
< The left version says: take the productive forces capitalism built, the automation and the logistics, and push them past what it can use. It's a plan about technology.
< It's what every technological transition has been. Nobody reformed their way from horses to engines.
< We know what the brake is made of. It's the same people it was last time.
> Capital is an inhuman
< A process that sets its own goals, routes around obstacles and reshapes its parts is an agent on any definition used for other systems.
< It decides where you live and what you study. For a spreadsheet it has a lot of say.
< They'd been building it and wanted to know what it was.
> Technology's growth
< Each of those was slowed in some places for some time. The claim is about the whole system under competition: what one group declines, another builds.
< Steered until somebody else needed it. Look at where each of those stands now.
< The people who own the last round of progress are the ones asking for the pause.
> Slowing down
< Delay costs lives too: cures arriving later, poverty lasting longer. A ledger that counts only the risks of acting is missing a column.
< Engineers ship. The ones who never ship aren't called careful.
< Posting while the thing gets built by people who read the posts.

## Byung-Chul Han
> We're a burnout society
< The boss is real. What's new is how many people with no boss in the room still can't stop, and call it passion.
< The email arrives at midnight and you answer it because you want to be the kind of person who does.
< Sixty pages is the form the argument takes about attention. He means it.
> Transparency destroys
< He's writing essays in a tradition that argues by concepts. The claim is that trust means not needing to see everything, and a demand for total visibility admits it's gone.
< Friendship, the confessional, the ballot booth. Some good things work because nobody's watching.
< It's clear, short sentences with no footnotes. You've confused opaque with uncited.
> Digital life
< More strangers, fewer others: the feed shows you people sorted to resemble you or to enrage you. An Other is someone you didn't select.
< Other people as content. That's what he means by abolished.
< Short enough to finish on the train and long enough to make you put the phone away for it.
> We've lost ritual
< He isn't asking for the past back. He's noting that repeated shared forms did something for attention and belonging that nothing has replaced.
< The servants had festivals, sabbaths and saints' days, which was more shared time off than you get.
< One short book a year, the same themes, turned slightly. He practices what he describes.

## Locke
> Mix your labor
< The labor argument comes with limits he states: enough and as good left for others, and nothing taken to spoil. The juice case ignores the first.
< You were paid for the fence. You sold the labor. That's in the theory.
< Whoever worked it and left enough for others. The second clause does a lot.
> Government rests
< Tacit consent is his weak point. The standard it sets isn't: a government that couldn't be justified to the people under it has no claim on them.
< Nobody asked, and that's the test your government is failing.
< Staying is the weak version and he knew it. Full membership, he says, takes express consent.
> There's a right to revolution
< He answers that the people judge, and that they're slow to it: they bear a long train of abuses before stirring. Rulers who break the trust are the rebels.
< Whoever wins is who decides under every theory. His says which side was in the right before the result.
< Men were being hanged for manuscripts that said less. Anonymous was prudent.
> Toleration
< The exclusions were about allegiance to a foreign power and oath-keeping. His own principle, that belief can't be forced, later overturned both.
< He excluded two groups on grounds that turned out to be false. The argument he built removed the exclusions.
< He did. The same texts were quoted by the people who ended it.

## Mill
> Power may be used
< He specifies it in practice: harm to interests that ought to be treated as rights. The principle rules out one class of reasons, the person's own good.
< Affecting someone isn't harming them. He draws the line at their protected interests, in chapter four.
< With the exceptions listed. Most people don't get as far as the list.
> Free discussion
< He never promises truth wins quickly. He says suppression assumes infallibility, and even true beliefs go dead when nobody may challenge them.
< And every correction of those falsehoods is on the same internet. Try the version with a censor.
< He saw the Victorian pamphlet wars. He'd recognize it.
> Experiments in living
< The harm principle already covers those cases: children and neighbors are others. The liberty is for what's left over.
< He meant that nobody knows in advance which ways of living are good. A lab coat fits fine.
< He drafted the company's defense, and its critics won. He then spent a career arguing for the losers of other fights.
> Better Socrates
< It's the best test available: ask those who know both. The alternative is a judge who knows one side only.
< The fool doesn't know what he's missing. That's the argument.
< He'd had a breakdown at twenty from a life of pure intellect, and poetry got him out. He'd tried both.

## Adam Smith
> The invisible hand
< That is the claim: under competition and with justice enforced, self-interest serves the public. The conditions are part of it, and he listed them.
< Burglary isn't exchange. He said self-interest within the rules of justice, and it's in the sentence.
< Once was enough. Newton states the law of gravity once too.
> The division of labor
< He saw the cost and prescribed the remedy in the same book: public education. Naming a trade-off and paying for the fix is what you want from an economist.
< Same book, and then he says what to do about it. That's the half his critics don't quote.
< Eighteen operations, ten men, forty-eight thousand pins a day. It's a good proof.
> Markets work best
< He's for markets freed from the privileges merchants arrange for themselves, with a state that does justice, defense and public works. The cartoon belongs to his fans.
< Smith is against monopoly, tariffs and merchants writing the law. That's still the right-wing position where there is one.
< The book is a thousand pages and every one of them is against somebody's subsidy.
> Sympathy
< The spectator is built from sympathy: we learn to see ourselves as others see us and correct for bias. The standard grows out of the feeling.
< We start with friends and learn to imagine a stranger watching. That's how anyone has ever become fair.
< And wrote two of the most important books of the century at her house.

## Hayek
> No planner
< Firms plan inside a market that tells them, through profit and loss, when the plan is wrong. Take that away and you have the plan without the correction.
< Walmart buys and sells at market prices and loses money when it guesses wrong. The prices are what it plans with.
< The software runs on prices set by rivals. Remove them and it has nothing to optimize.
> Economic planning
< The book warns about central direction of production. He says in it that a safety net and regulation are compatible with freedom, and Europe built those.
< Sweden has free trade, private firms and school vouchers. That's the road he recommended.
< He supported a basic minimum and health insurance in that book. Britain nationalized steel and reversed it.
> Social order emerges
< He includes law in the order: common-law rules grown case by case, which no one drafted whole. Legislation tidies what custom found.
< The rulebook records practices traders had already worked out. It came after.
< The laws were mostly writing down what people were already doing.
> "Social justice"
< We can judge and change rules. His point is that calling an unplanned outcome "unjust" implies a culprit, and it licenses someone to assign shares.
< Traffic deaths get reduced by rules that apply to everyone. He's for that. He's against deciding who should arrive first.
< It was a bad thing to say. His argument against central planning doesn't depend on it.

## Arendt
> Evil can be banal
< She may have misread Eichmann. The thesis doesn't need him: most of the people who made it run were clerks, drivers and schedulers who never asked.
< A true believer who talks entirely in clichés and office-speak is still her specimen.
< Take him out and the camps were still staffed by thousands who were only doing a job.
> Totalitarianism
< She compares structures: terror as a method, ideology as logic, the camps as the central institution. The two differed in content and matched on those.
< Tyrants wanted obedience. These wanted to remake what a person is, and built institutions for it.
< She gave reasons, at five hundred pages.
> Politics is action
< The distinction protects something: a space where people decide together as equals, which administration by experts can't supply. She misapplied it in places.
< Who gets what can be settled by a spreadsheet. Whether we're the kind of people who settle it that way is politics.
< Athens for the form. She's explicit that the question is how to have it without the exclusions.
> Thinking is what guards
< She means the habit of stopping to ask whether you could live with yourself afterwards. It differs from intelligence, and the clever joined in numbers.
< Doctorates measure cleverness. She says cleverness is no protection at all.
< That's why she drew the distinction. She'd seen the cleverest man she knew fail it.

## Carl Schmitt
> Sovereign is he
< Emergency powers written into law still need someone to say when they apply and when they end. That decision can't be fully written down in advance.
< Every coup leader agrees because it describes what happens. A theory isn't refuted by who finds it accurate.
< It's a theory of what's underneath the rules on the day they're tested.
> Politics is the distinction
< He says it's the limiting case that gives politics its seriousness. Bargaining goes on in the shadow of the possibility that it fails.
< The council's decision is enforced, in the end, by police. Parking is downstream of who has that power.
< High school has no monopoly on force. That's the missing ingredient.
> Liberalism only postpones
< His claim is narrower: when the divide is over what kind of state to be, procedure can't settle it, because the procedure is what's in dispute.
< The other option showed the diagnosis right and the cure monstrous. Both are true.
< He described a parliament that couldn't govern in 1923. It then failed to.
> His diagnosis
< What he did is on the record and it was vile. Thinkers on the left and right still use the analysis because it describes what constitutional texts leave out.
< Applied by a careerist with no decency. The theory says someone decides. It doesn't say they'll be any good.
< Liberal lawyers assign him to learn what a constitution has to be defended against.

## Nationalism
> Every nation deserves
< The principle is self-rule for peoples who want it. Where populations mix, it argues for autonomy and federation, and the worst cases came from denying both.
< The map was drawn in pen by empires. Nationalism is the people on it asking to be consulted.
< The line is where one group of people agreed to pay for each other's schools.
> Nations are ancient
< The modern form is recent. It was built from shared languages, stories and territory much older than the railway.
< The kilt pattern is new. The language, the songs and the fact of being ruled from elsewhere weren't.
< Invented the way a house is: by people, from materials already on the site.
> We owe more
< The priority comes from shared institutions: you pay into the same system and live under the same law. It's the same kind of tie as family, at larger scale.
< He and the man four hundred miles north fund each other's hospitals. The river comes with a treasury.
< It's the belief that your family is yours, extended. Nobody demands a ranking for that.
> Shared identity
< Class and religion divide a country as readily as they unite it. A national "we" is the one that can include both sides of those.
< The strong welfare states were built when national feeling was high and trust followed it.
< Every "we" has an edge. Yours does too, and it just isn't on a map.

## Social democracy
> Capitalism can be tamed
< The conditions changed and the model adapted: more training, portable benefits, public childcare. The countries that kept it are still the best places to be poor.
< Capital threatens to leave and mostly stays where the workers are skilled and the courts work.
< An apology with paid parental leave and a pension attached.
> The Nordic model
< The parts transfer separately: bargaining by sector, active job programs, universal services. Countries with none of the supposed prerequisites have adopted pieces and they worked.
< Four of the five have no oil to speak of. Trust rose after the programs came in.
< The Danes complain constantly. They vote for it anyway.
> High taxes
< That is the honest version, and its advocates say it: everyone pays in and everyone gets it back in services. Broad taxes are why it's stable.
< It's in the brochure. Everyone pays and everyone gets childcare. The middle class comes out ahead.
< Then the honest half is the taxes. Some of us do say so.
> Reform beats
< Reforms that become universal are hard to reverse: nobody has abolished a public pension or a health service once people had it.
< The sandcastle is eighty years old and has a health service in it.
< A hundred years in which the working week halved and children left the mines. That'll do.

## Centrism
> The truth is usually
< The claim isn't that you average. It's that each camp tends to be right about what the other ignores, so a good policy usually has parts from both.
< If one says four and the other says six, check the sum. The centrist is the one who checks.
< Strong opinions about tax credits and zoning. They're just boring ones.
> Both sides
< It's a starting presumption, dropped when the evidence says so. The alternative is assuming one side has no point before looking.
< One was wrong, and working out which took listening to both.
< It's what people say when they've read both and found each leaving something out.
> Moderation
< In a divided country a compromise that sticks beats a good policy repealed in four years. Durability is part of whether a policy is good.
< Half a bridge is a bad compromise. A bridge with a toll neither side wanted is a normal one.
< Done badly and still in place twenty years later. Check what happened to the pure versions.
> Extremes
< The resemblance is in methods: contempt for procedure, an enemy inside, an end that excuses means. Those matter whatever the goal.
< Abolitionists won through elections, courts and an amendment. Sure of the end, moderate in the means.
< It explains who ends up attacking the same courts and the same journalists.

## Technocracy

## Meritocracy
> Positions should go
< Head starts are an argument for fixing the start. They aren't an argument for handing the scalpel to someone who can't operate.
< And the alternative is the children of the well-connected with no exam at all. That was the previous system.
< An exam anyone may sit beats a title you have to be born with.
> Success reflects
< Luck sets the range and effort decides where in it you land. Policy can reward only the part people control, and that's a reason to reward it.
< He did, and the pay gap is the problem being pointed at. That's an argument about wages.
< Nobody earns their parents. What you do with the start is still yours.
> Equal opportunity
< It's a direction, and it's measurable: how far a parent's income predicts a child's. Some countries have cut that in half.
< So move the start. Nobody's proposal for fixing the race is to stop timing it.
< Free to compete, and in the countries that fund the schools, a lot of them win.
> A meritocratic elite
< The smugness is real and fixable. Compare the alternatives on results: selection by birth, by party loyalty or by lot.
< The old aristocrats knew it was luck and kept the land anyway.
< The author was warning about an elite chosen by test alone. People who read it added other checks.

## Georgism
> Land value belongs
< Assessors already value land separately in several places. And a tax can be phased in over decades, which protects current owners.
< Phase it in and let her defer the bill until the house is sold. This was worked out long ago.
< It's one idea that covers housing, sprawl, speculation and taxes on work. Most movements would like one of those.
> A single tax
< Fair, the single-tax claim is too strong at today's budgets. Shifting what we can from wages and buildings onto land is the live proposal.
< Land values rose along with government. Urban land is now the largest asset class there is.
< Pittsburgh, Denmark, Singapore, Taiwan and Hong Kong have all used versions. Where it's been tried it's hard to get repealed.
> A land tax has no deadweight
< Imperfect assessment means some error. Every other tax has the error and also discourages work or building. A tax can't reduce the supply of land.
< Assuming rough anything. A sloppy land tax still can't make there be less land.
< It'll happen when the people who'd pay it stop being the ones who vote on it.
> Land speculation
< He meant it as the reason progress and poverty arrive together: gains in productivity end up in rents. For cities now, the data fit.
< And when the landlords arrived, the ones doing the work started paying to stand on the ground.
< The inventor wrote two sets of rules. The cooperative set is the one that got dropped.

## Neoreaction
> Democracy is a failed
< The record belongs to rich countries with strong institutions, several of which were built under monarchs. The claim is about where the present trend leads.
< He'd live in the ones still running on capital built up before the franchise widened. That's the argument.
< A blog was enough for Luther.
> A sovereign CEO
< The design includes owners who can replace the executive. The proposal is to make government answer to someone with a stake in its long-run value.
< There's a board in the proposal. People skip the corporate governance section.
< A country can't fail quietly either way. A structure where somebody owns the downside is the point.
> "The Cathedral"
< It's a claim about incentives: nobody coordinates, and the career rewards in universities and press point the same way. That predicts which errors go uncorrected.
< A lot of people who disagree with him, who all hire and promote each other.
< They publish him as a curiosity. Watch what happens to staff who agree.
> People should exit
< Exit disciplines governments the way it disciplines firms. Lowering what it costs to leave is the program, and it would help the poor most.
< "Leave" is how most people have improved their lot. Ask an immigrant.
< Most of his ancestors were peasants, like everyone's. They did better under some kings than others.

## Pacifism
> Violence is never
< Most pacifists reject war and killing and accept restraint and defense short of that. And the tradition is full of people who went unarmed into the danger.
< The pacifist stands in the doorway. Several are remembered for doing just that.
< It also works when enough people are. That's how most wars have been ended.
> Nonviolent resistance
< In studies of campaigns over a century, nonviolent ones succeeded about twice as often as violent ones, including against dictatorships.
< It also worked in Poland, the Philippines, Chile and East Germany, against secret police and without the newspapers.
< He was wrong about that case. The record across a hundred others isn't.
> War only breeds
< The second war grew out of the settlement of the first. The peace that held came from occupation, aid and rebuilding.
< Reconstruction bred it. The same countries after the first war got a punitive treaty and twenty years.
< Read on a chapter. Most of them are the opening of the next one.
> Refusing to fight
< Purity isn't the claim. It's a refusal to be the one who kills, and someone has to hold that line or nobody does.
< Medics, stretcher-bearers and relief workers were on that fire, unarmed.
< A good number were jailed, beaten or shot for it. Brave is right.

## Free speech
> The answer to bad speech
< The alternative is an official who decides what's a lie. That power has been used against the truth at least as often as for it.
< Slower than the lie and faster than a ministry.
< Censorship rules are written by people with platforms too. The powerless are who they've been used on.
> Truth wins out
< He never promised quick victory. The claim is that no one can be trusted to pick the winner in advance, and open contest corrects error over time.
< The pet rock is gone. That's the market working.
< And an astronomy shelf nobody had to ban the horoscopes to stock.
> Any restriction
< Those limits are narrow and old, tied to concrete injury. The slope worry is about the new category: opinions restricted for being false or hurtful.
< The old limits held because they're tied to a specific injury. The new ones are tied to how someone feels, and that has no edge.
< A short list fixed in advance is the whole idea. A list that grows by complaint is something else.
> Offense is not harm
< Threats and harassment are conduct and already covered. What's left is being confronted with a view you hate, and that is the cost of living among free people.
< He's furious and he's posting back. That's the system.
< Blocking is choosing not to listen. That was always allowed.

## Universal basic income
> A basic income would end
< A modest amount paid to everyone, clawed back through the tax system, costs far less than the headline figure. Most people are paying themselves.
< Subtract what the same people pay in tax and the net figure is a fraction of that.
< Every pilot of anything is. That's how you find out before spending the real money.
> People won't stop
< Alaska has paid a permanent dividend for forty years and employment there looks like anywhere else. The long experiments show small effects.
< Alaska's has run for forty years with no end date. They kept their jobs.
< They look after children, study and start businesses. That's what the data show for the hours given up.
> It's simpler
< It replaces the cash programs and the forms that go with them. Disability and housing support stay, with one fewer layer of means-testing underneath.
< It replaces the programs with a caseworker and a waiting list. Those are most of the paperwork.
< Each has read it and likes a different half. That's what a coalition is.
> Automation
< It may not be needed for that reason. The case stands without the robots: the bargaining power to refuse a bad job is worth having now.
< The loom took the weavers' jobs and their grandchildren did fine. The weavers didn't.
< A tip jar funded by the people who replaced your job is a start.
