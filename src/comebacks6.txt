# Theory, Political Thinkers, Politics (the original sixteen).

## Foucault
> Power produces
< He never says knowledge is nothing but power. He says the two come together, in clinics and prisons and surveys, and that what gets studied depends on who can be made to hold still.
< The powerful don't know the most. They decide what counts as knowing: who gets examined, filed and graded. That's the claim.
< He found it in the timetable, the exam and the case file. You were looking for a villain.
> Madness and sexuality
< He doesn't say the suffering is unreal. He says where the line fell, and who policed it, has a history. The archival fights are over dates.
< A broken leg has been a broken leg in every century. Homosexuality was a sin, then a disease, then neither. Only one of those needs a historian.
< The historians checked them and then started writing history his way.
> Modern discipline
< It's a diagram. Its principle is being visible without knowing when you're watched, so you watch yourself. That needs no round building.
< You want your kid to get in. Having a subject want it is how the thing works.
< Nobody had to. There's a camera on your street and a rating on your profile.
> There's no human nature
< His reply was that the idea of justice Chomsky appeals to was itself shaped by the society it would judge. That's a caution about the standard, and not a refusal to oppose anything.
< They come out the same every time? Read two centuries apart on madness or sex and say that again.
< He said the task was to criticize institutions that look neutral. He then went and organized on behalf of prisoners.
> Critique doesn't
< Genealogy removes a practice's claim to be natural and necessary. Once it looks optional, the people subject to it can ask whether they want it.
< "Interesting" in a suspicious voice is how every reform started. Somebody noticed the thing wasn't inevitable.
< He'd agree, and tell you to write the genealogy of it.

## Derrida & postmodernism
> There is nothing outside
< The mild reading is the right one and he said so many times. That it's uncontroversial once stated correctly isn't a mark against it.
< The lease is a text. So are the notice and the law that backs it. You picked the example with the most paperwork.
< A check is a text with a signature, and he wrote a whole essay about signatures.
> Meaning is endlessly
< He doesn't deny that people communicate. He denies that any context can be closed for good, which is why contracts get litigated and scripture has commentaries.
< You understood it well enough for now. Ask again in a hundred years. That's "deferred."
< And then finding out what follows from that. You stopped at the dictionary.
> Binary oppositions
< He doesn't say every ranking is wrong. He shows that the first term keeps needing the second to define itself, so the ranking isn't the simple thing it claimed to be.
< "On" is defined by a circuit that includes "off." You can't have the top without the bottom. The switch is his example, working.
< Sokal fooled an unrefereed journal. The same trick has since worked on physics and medicine journals.
> Be suspicious
< He called it a report on what had already happened to belief, and he qualified it later. Describing a loss of faith isn't founding a new church.
< A weather report that the big storms are over isn't a big storm.
< It says truths have histories. People who repeat "there's no truth" are the ones misquoting.
> The obscure style
< Where the claim is that ordinary phrasing hides an assumption, writing in ordinary phrases would repeat it. The style is an argument, and you can judge it as one.
< The guides sell because readers want to get to him. Nobody writes a guide to a writer who's finished.
< That story comes from Searle, who was not a neutral party.

## Deleuze
> Philosophy is the creation
< The test is what a concept lets you do: which problems it makes thinkable. He says a concept is good the way a tool is good, and that's a standard.
< Conspiracy forums recycle one concept, the hidden hand. Making a new one is the hard part.
< He was. The others say they're discovering what they invented.
> Difference comes before
< The view from identity treats difference as what's left after comparing two finished things. His claim is that the things are produced by differing, like a gradient before there are two sides.
< Different from what it was a moment ago. No second thing required.
< Each time it comes back it's changed. That's the title, and you read it.
> The rhizome
< He says trees and rhizomes turn into each other and the pair isn't a ranking. It's a way of noticing when a branching model is being forced on a network.
< Ginger has no trunk and no top, and any piece grows a new plant. That's the property. He picked the right vegetable.
< It's written so you can start on any chapter. That was announced on page one.
> Desire is productive
< He criticizes desires by what they do: whether they connect and expand or close down. That's a standard, and it replaces "lack" with "blockage."
< Hunger builds farms, kitchens, trade routes and cuisines. It's the most productive thing people have.
< They produce. That's the entire point of calling them machines.
> The math
< The borrowed terms are used as concepts. He says philosophy takes from science without doing science. Judge the concept by its use.
< Mathematicians have read him and written books on his use of the calculus. Zero is not the count.
< A bag is organized. Read it again.

## Žižek
> Ideology is strongest
< The claim is specific: we now know very well and act as if we didn't. That can be checked against behavior, and it describes how most people use their phones.
< Reading Žižek and feeling safely outside is the exact move he describes. So yes. He'd agree and include himself.
< He says he's in it too. That's most of the act.
> Lacan plus Hegel
< The same structure turning up in many places is what a theory of ideology predicts. The test is whether each reading shows something the film's makers would recognize.
< Hegel can be read, and you've used the word "alienation" this week.
< Wrong about the plot, right about why you liked it.
> The jokes
< A joke that works shows the structure it relies on. If you laughed, you already saw the mechanism, and the explaining comes after.
< The good comedians are theorists. Ask one how a joke about class works and you'll get twenty minutes.
< He says he repeats himself. It's the only way to find out what you actually think.
> He's a serious communist
< He says the old program failed and the first duty is not to rush into the next one. Refusing to supply a blueprint on demand is a position about blueprints.
< He takes capitalism's money to attack capitalism. That's the contradiction he lectures on, performed live.
< Dangerous and booked. That's what the system does to everything, as he keeps saying.
> The provocations
< The reversal is the method when the audience's expectation is the ideology. It fails as argument only if the reversed claim can't be defended, and he usually tries.
< If the opposite is true in every room, the rooms have a problem.
< And so on, and so on. He knows.

## Psychoanalysis
> The unconscious drives
< The modern findings cover the core: motives people can't report, defenses, the long reach of early attachment. The specific mechanisms have been revised, as in any field.
< The analyst can't check, and the patient can: something shifts when the guess is right. That's the test, session by session.
< And when you rolled your eyes just now, whose voice was that in?
> Repression and the Oedipus
< The literal family drama is the weakest part. The general claim survives: a child's first loves and rivalries leave a pattern that later ones repeat.
< "It's fine" is the most examined sentence in the history of the couch.
< Sophocles had it packing theaters two thousand years before Freud.
> Resisting
< Good practice never took a "no" as confirmation. An interpretation is tested by what follows it: new memories, a change in the symptom, or nothing.
< He doesn't own the coin. The patient can leave. They mostly come back, and that's also data.
< Freud wrote the paper saying yes and no both prove nothing. You're attacking a position he rejected in 1937.
> Clinical experience
< The long-term outcome studies now exist, and the gains from this kind of therapy keep growing after it ends. That's a measurable difference.
< Bloodletting never had a patient say "that's it" and stop having the symptom.
< The follow-up on the pills isn't flattering either.
> Lacan made Freud rigorous
< The formulas are shorthand for teaching, and he said they weren't mathematics. The rigor is in reading Freud line by line against what patients say.
< He meant it's made of substitution and combination, like metaphor and metonymy. He used French, heavily.
< A session ends when something has been said. Sometimes that takes five minutes. Sometimes it takes you ten years.

## Marx
> History is the history
< He doesn't claim class is the only cause. Other struggles take the shape that material interests allow, and the long sweep from feudalism to capitalism fits that.
< Follow the church's land and the princes' revenues through those wars and say that again.
< With a mortgage. He covered that. It's called a chain.
> Labor is the source
< The exploitation claim survives without it: one group owns the means of production, the other must sell its time, and the surplus goes to the first.
< He says in chapter one that useless labor creates no value. You've refuted page ten with something on page eleven.
< He read every factory inspector's report in the British Museum for thirty years. The inspectors are the ones in the footnotes.
> Capitalism's contradictions
< The crises came on schedule. What he missed was how far the state would go to manage them, and that's a revision the theory can take.
< 1873, 1929, 1973, 2008. It keeps having the heart attack and getting the paddles.
< And it's still the best description of last quarter.
> Real Marxism
< He expected revolution in rich industrial countries with a majority working class. It was tried in poor agrarian ones under siege, which he said couldn't work.
< All forty were poor, rural and at war. The sample has one kind of country in it.
< "That wasn't real capitalism" is what gets said after every crash.
> Ideas reflect
< He says ideas are conditioned, and that members of a class can see past its interests when its position is crumbling. He names himself as an instance.
< A factory owner's money paying for a book against factory owners isn't the ideology you'd expect. That's the anomaly the theory explains.
< Yes. Best use of surplus value on record.

## Socialism
> Workers should control
< Co-ops that get going survive as long as other firms and pay better. The obstacle is at the start: banks lend against ownership, and workers have none.
< A form and a bank account, and then a loan no bank will make to people with no collateral. The clerk isn't the obstacle.
< With what? The bottom half owns about one percent of it.
> Planning can beat
< The modern proposal isn't one ministry. It's public ownership with markets or with democratic planning by sector, and every large firm already plans internally.
< They also put the first satellite up and taught a country to read in a generation. The soap was a choice of priorities.
< Amazon decides how many shoes, for every size in every city. It can be done.
> The Nordic
< They got there through the strongest unions on earth and decades of socialist governments. Markets were part of it, and so was the labor movement the label refers to.
< With union density above sixty percent and a welfare state he'd have to pay into. Take the billionaires and the bargaining together.
< He asked Americans to stop. The party that built it is called the Social Democrats.
> Inequality is injustice
< Enough for everyone would be a start. Large gaps also buy political power and turn into unequal chances for the next generation, so the gap matters too.
< Nobody's height comes out of anybody else's. Wealth gets made in a workplace, by more than one person.
< Most socialists have something. They want to keep it and want you to have it too.
> Human needs
< Profit tracks what people can pay for. Insulin and yachts send different signals, and that's the complaint.
< Bakeries don't feed people who can't pay. That gap gets filled by somebody not making a profit.
< It's when workers own things. The government part is your definition.

## Capitalism
> Markets allocate
< Those failures are the textbook exceptions and each has a market-compatible fix: price the pollution, disclose the risk. Every other system fails without any exception clause.
< Marketing is information about what exists. The alternative is one kind of soap and a queue.
< And the planned economies needed a bailout from 1989 onward.
> Capitalism lifted
< China's poverty fell when it let farmers sell their crops and firms keep profits. The state was there before 1978 too, and so was the poverty.
< Poverty fell fastest exactly where and when markets opened, country by country. Smoking didn't follow that schedule.
< The line for the poorest billion went up most of all. That's whose.
> Wages reflect
< Not perfectly. Over time and across countries, pay follows productivity closely, and the gaps people point to are mostly cases where competition is being blocked.
< One manager's decision moves a billion dollars of other people's savings. A mistake that size is worth paying to avoid.
< He was paid for the rockets and the cars. The posting is free, and you get what you pay for.
> Free exchange
< The bad options aren't made by the employer who adds one. Taking away the offer leaves the person with the same need and one choice fewer.
< The mugger takes away an option you had. The employer adds one you didn't. That's the whole structure.
< In every other system they also had to eat, and there was less of it.
> Competition drives
< The research was public and sat on shelves. Turning it into something a billion people can afford took firms that would go broke if they got it wrong.
< The government had all those parts for decades and didn't make a phone.
< The phone in your hand would have been a supercomputer in 1995. Complain on it.

## Rawls & liberalism
> Justice is what
< The veil is a device for showing what fairness requires, and he says so. The stipulations are argued for one by one, since they express conditions people accept on reflection.
< It's a test, like asking how you'd cut the cake if someone else picked first. You don't need to have cut it.
< A Harvard professor who concluded Harvard professors shouldn't get more unless the janitor does better for it.
> Inequalities must benefit
< Extreme caution fits a one-time choice of basic institutions with your whole life at stake. And the principle applies to the basic structure, leaving individual transfers alone.
< "Any inequality" has to be the arrangement that leaves the poorest best off out of all the possibilities. One dollar almost never passes.
< No Democratic platform has ever come close to it.
> The state should be neutral
< He dropped neutrality of effect. The claim is neutrality of justification: basic law shouldn't rest on one contested account of the good life.
< It can say why. People who disagree about the good still have to share a state, and neutrality is the term each can accept.
< Welcome so long as it doesn't use the police to make the others join. That's the one condition.
> Laws must be justifiable
< "Reasonable" means willing to offer terms others could accept and to admit that sincere people disagree. It leaves out those who'd impose, whatever their doctrine, liberals included.
< Reasonable people agree to look for terms. They don't agree on the answer. That gap is the whole theory.
< It's defined in the text. Twice. It earned tenure.
> Natural talents
< He doesn't say talents aren't yours. He says no one deserves their starting place, so the rules for rewarding talent need a justification other than desert.
< He agreed. He said his career was mostly luck and wouldn't take credit for it in interviews.
< A theorist who revises under criticism. You say that as if it's a flaw.

## Libertarianism
> Taxation is theft
< If rights were only what law defines, nothing a legal system did to property could be unjust. People do judge confiscations unjust, so some claim comes first.
< Slavery was legal. Legal isn't the test of whether something is taken by force.
< I use the road because it's the only one they allowed. Using the monopoly isn't endorsing it.
> Self-ownership
< The acquisition problem is real, and every theory of property has one. This one at least starts from a premise most people accept: that nobody else owns you.
< You can also choose not to sell. A right you may waive is still a right. You can give away your books too.
< And the state's rule is fine so long as the state decides what counts as a crime.
> Only the minimal state
< Whatever one makes of the derivation, the conclusion stands as a burden: each further function has to be justified to the people forced to pay for it.
< Men with guns who aren't permitted to do anything but stop other men with guns. The limit is the freedom part.
< And what you own. Courts work for tenants too.
> Markets would solve
< Many of those failures have property-rights fixes, and regulators fail as well, often at the request of the firms they regulate. Compare real markets with real agencies.
< The chalk ended when buyers could sue and newspapers could print brand names. Liability law did that.
< Somalia had warlords, which is several governments. Its phone network did improve, as it happens.
> Liberty is the absence
< Poverty is a serious evil and it's a different one. Folding it into "unfreedom" lets coercion be justified as liberation, which is how that argument has usually been used.
< Free and dead of thirst are different words. He's free. He's also in trouble. Both are true and only one is my topic.
< The kid is why the tax bill matters more.

## Anarchism
> No one has the right
< Procedures bind those who took part in them. Anarchists don't reject organization or decisions. They reject orders backed by force from people you can't recall.
< People settled which side of the road by convention before any traffic law existed. That's coordination. Ruling is the fine.
< The second person is a neighbor. You're thinking of the third one, with the badge.
> Mutual aid
< Scale is the open problem, and federation is the proposed answer: small units, recallable delegates. Large voluntary networks exist, from the Red Cross to open-source software.
< The group project fails when a teacher assigned the groups. People who chose each other finish.
< An HOA has a board, fines and a lien on your house. That's the thing we're against.
> Catalonia
< Being crushed by two armies and betrayed by a third isn't evidence it didn't work. While it stood, factories and farms ran and output held.
< It lost to Franco, Hitler's air force and Stalin's police at once. That's a strong opponent list for a pilot program.
< Ten months in which several million people ran a region. Your system needed a century to get the trains running.
> Hierarchy
< The test is the one stated: authority has to justify itself to those under it and be removable. A surgeon passes. A landlord has to make the case.
< The patient picked the surgeon and can fire him. That's the kind of authority anarchists have always accepted.
< He rotates out next month. That's the difference between a facilitator and a boss.
> Property is theft
< He distinguished possession, what you use and live in, from property, a title to charge others for what you don't use. Possession is the one he called freedom.
< It was held in common before the first fence. You can steal from everyone.
< The bike is a possession. The building it's chained to is the property.

## Hobbes
> Without a sovereign
< Those societies are small and their homicide rates, where measured, run high. His claim is about strangers at scale with nothing to enforce agreements.
< They lived in bands of fifty where everyone was a cousin. Try it with eight million strangers.
< He lived through a civil war. The fear was a field observation.
> Rational people
< Divided power is how the war he lived through began: two authorities each claiming the last word. A lion is one danger and a pack is many.
< The exit clause is the state of nature. You can always go back. People don't.
< One you can predict beats a thousand you can't.
> Human beings
< He needs less than egoism: only that enough people may be dangerous and nobody can tell which. Distrust alone gets the argument going.
< He wrote it for his own safety and yours. They happened to coincide. That's the book.
< You lock your door at night. Who do you think is out there, by your account?
> There's no justice
< His answer is that the man who breaks faith can't be let into any group that knows it, so he's betting his life on never being found out.
< Promises count once there's something to make them count. That's the thing being built.
< You renew it every day you call the police instead of a cousin.
> Order matters
< He never says the sovereign is safe. He says the alternative is worse, and the worst modern death rates do come from places where the state collapsed.
< A cemetery is what you get without order. That was his whole point.
< It's a long way of saying what happens when nobody can tell anyone anything.

## Rousseau
> Man is born free
< He says outright that the state of nature is a hypothesis for separating what's natural from what's made. The claim it supports is that existing chains are no fact of nature.
< He means no one is born a subject or a master. The baby's neck was never the claim.
< He said so himself, in print, with shame. The argument doesn't depend on his being fit to make it.
> The general will
< He gives conditions: general laws only, no factions, informed citizens, rough equality. Where they fail he says the vote doesn't express it. Those are demanding standards.
< It's found by voting. The vote is the procedure. People who skipped the voting weren't using his idea.
< It means the law you gave yourself can be enforced on you. Every speed limit does it.
> Civilization corrupts
< The later books refine it. Progress in knowledge and comfort is real, and it came with dependence, vanity and inequality. He's asking about the cost.
< He won an essay contest by saying essay contests make people worse. He knew. That's the joke, and he was in on it.
< They took him in, lionized him, then threw stones at his house. That's about what he predicted.
> Property is the origin
< He separates natural differences from the kind that needs other people's agreement. Status among foragers isn't rent, debt or inheritance.
< And then needed guards for it. More food and the first guard arrived in the same season.
< Voltaire made a joke. That was his method with everything he couldn't answer.
> Education should follow
< The tutor arranges the world so the child meets things before words about them. All teaching arranges something, and this kind hides the hand so the child reasons.
< Eating dirt once is how a child learns about dirt. That's the method.
< He says in the book that a man who can't do a father's duty has no right to be one. He knew who he meant.

## Conservatism
> Tradition embodies
< The claim is a presumption: what has survived generations of trial has passed a test no new plan has. The burden sits with the reformer, and it can be met.
< And the tradition absorbed the changes that worked. That's the filter running.
< Somebody has to check which of them were mistakes. You're welcome.
> Change should be gradual
< Existing harms are known and bounded. The harms of a sweeping change aren't, and they can't be undone. Preferring the reversible step is a view about risk.
< He put it up because the cattle kept getting out. Somebody remembers. Ask before you pull it.
< A history degree is about four years. The fence took four centuries.
> Free markets
< The tension is real, and conservatives have said so for a century. Family, church and local ties are what make a market society livable, which is why they get defended.
< People buy tradition every December, and at every wedding and funeral.
< A short list. Yours is longer, it just has different things on it.
> Burke was right
< He'd defended the Americans, the Irish and India against his own government. His objection was to tearing down every institution at once in the name of a theory.
< France took five republics, two empires and a terror to get there. Britain reformed and skipped that part.
< He predicted the Terror, the wars and a general in 1790. That's three.
> Society is a pact
< It works as a restraint on the living: you aren't the owner of what you inherited, only a tenant with duties in both directions.
< The unborn are exactly who gets the bill for whatever's decided today. They're the most affected party in the room.
< Three hundred years of losing slowly is called surviving.

## Machiavelli
> It's better to be feared
< Yes, that's the actual view, and it's better than the slogan: fear without hatred, cruelty used once and briefly, and the people's support as the only real fortress.
< Loved rulers get poisoned by the people closest to them. He ran the numbers on Rome, emperor by emperor.
< He lost it for serving a republic loyally. Then he wrote down what he'd seen.
> Politics should be judged
< He means the survival of the state and the safety of the people in it. A ruler with clean hands who loses the city has failed the people he was responsible for.
< He kept Florence's republic alive for fourteen years with no army and no money. The exile came when someone else's troops arrived.
< The best cover letter ever written. Still read after five hundred years.
> The Prince is realism
< Agreed, it isn't satire. It describes what holding power requires so that it can be done with the least cruelty. The moralizing manuals around it described nothing.
< It means what it says: this is how it's done. Not liking that is different from finding a secret meaning.
< In hardcover, and so do the people who say it's evil. Somebody's reading it.
> Skill can master
< He says fortune rules about half of what we do. Borgia is the example of how much skill achieves and where it stops, and the chapter says both.
< He built a state out of nothing in three years and was undone by falling ill the week his father died. That's the half that's fortune.
< At 31 he'd done more than most dynasties.
> He founded
< The step was the question he asked: what actually happens, and what follows from it. Earlier writers described rulers as they ought to be.
< Cases are data. He compared Rome, Sparta, Venice and Florence on the same questions, which is the method.
< And of the six people. They read it to see what he's up to.

## Democracy
> The people should rule
< No voting rule is perfect, and that's a result about every way of deciding together, juntas and committees included. Elections remain the one where losers accept the result.
< Athens also lasted two centuries, beat Persia and invented most of what you're citing. Two bad votes in two hundred years.
< Five minutes with the average king would settle that one.
> Voters are capable
< Voters lack detail and still do the one job that matters: noticing when things go badly and removing those in charge. No other system manages that.
< The passenger doesn't fly the plane. He picks the airline and stops using it after a crash. That's the job.
< Half of every profession is below average, including the one that writes about voters.
> Democracy is
< Those regimes do well under a good ruler and catastrophically under the next one. The case is the worst outcome avoided, and averages hide it.
< Every alternative has been tried, for most of history, by most of the world. That's why they stopped.
< It's a joke with a ninety-year track record.
> Elections make
< Even crude punishment is feedback. Leaders who know they can be thrown out for a bad year behave differently from those who can't be.
< They win because they vote the way the district wants. Losing is the threat that makes them.
< And then you can fire one of them. Try that with the other kind.
> Majority rule
< Rights and courts are part of modern democracy, adopted by supermajorities. Binding yourself in advance is something majorities chose to do.
< Under every other system it's one wolf and nobody counts.
< 51% can be wrong. They can also be outvoted next time. That's the feature.
