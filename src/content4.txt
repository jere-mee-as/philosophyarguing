# Second wave: more philosophies and philosophers. Same format, four claims each.

## Heraclitus | Ancients
> Everything flows
If nothing stayed the same there'd be nothing for the change to happen to. Talk of change needs something that persists through it.
If everything flows, so does the claim that everything flows. By Tuesday it's a different claim, and nobody has to answer the old one.
Heraclitus said nothing stays the same, and people have been quoting the same line for 2,500 years.
> You can't step into the same river twice
The river is the same river because rivers are the kind of thing that consists of moving water. Different water was never a different river.
The Nile is on every map since there were maps. Same place, same name, same river. Cartography settled this.
You can step in the same river twice. People have summer houses on it.
> Strife is justice; war is the father of all
That opposites are in tension doesn't make conflict good or generative. Plenty of tension just destroys things.
War is the father of ruins. Everything that's still standing is standing because nobody fought near it.
"War is the father of all" is a thing said by a man who never left Ephesus.
> There's a logos, and most people sleep through it
A principle that only the speaker grasps, stated in riddles, can't be checked by anyone else. Obscurity isn't evidence of depth.
If most people can't hear the logos, the simplest explanation is that it isn't making a sound.
They called him "the Obscure" in his own lifetime. It wasn't a compliment then either.

## Parmenides & Zeno | Ancients
> Change is impossible: what is, is
The argument treats "is not" as naming a thing, nothing, and then says it can't be spoken of. Saying something isn't red doesn't refer to nothingness.
He wrote a poem to prove it. Before the poem there was no poem. Then there was.
Parmenides proved nothing changes and then changed philosophy, apparently.
> Achilles never catches the tortoise
An infinite series of shrinking intervals can have a finite sum. The calculus answers the arithmetic, whatever puzzles remain about space.
Achilles is faster than a tortoise. That's the whole problem and the whole solution.
Zeno proved motion is impossible, and someone answered by standing up and walking. Still the best reply.
> Reason overrides the senses
When a chain of reasoning ends in "nothing moves," the more reasonable step is to doubt a premise. Conclusions can discredit arguments.
Reason is done by a brain, which he found out about using his senses.
When the argument says the tortoise wins and the eyes say otherwise, one of them has a problem, and it isn't the eyes.
> Nothing comes from nothing
As a principle about causes it's plausible, but it doesn't follow that there's no change. Things come from other things.
Every idea Parmenides had came from nothing. He'd say so himself, he was very proud they were original.
Zeno's paradoxes are word problems that got out of hand.

## Pythagoras | Ancients
> All is number
That numbers describe things doesn't make things numbers. The map and the territory get merged in one sentence.
My landlord is not a number. I've checked, he has a car.
Pythagoras thought everything was number, and then the square root of two showed up and they reportedly drowned the guy who told people.
> The soul passes from body to body
There's no evidence of memory or character carrying over, and without those it's unclear what it would mean for it to be the same soul.
The population has gone up by billions. Either souls are being manufactured or the theory has an inventory problem.
He said he recognized a dead friend's voice in a dog's bark. That's the evidence.
> Mathematics purifies the soul
Mathematical skill and moral character come apart all the time. Whatever proof does for the mind, it isn't a moral training.
If math purified the soul, accountants would be the holiest people alive.
The Pythagoreans wouldn't eat beans. Founders of rational thought.
> The heavens move in harmony
The planets' motions don't fit musical ratios. Kepler tried for years to make them, and what survived of that work was the ellipses.
Space is a vacuum. Sound doesn't travel in a vacuum. The music of the spheres is, physically, nothing.
He didn't prove the theorem. The Babylonians had it a thousand years earlier. He had the cult.

## Epicurus | Ancients
> Pleasure is the highest good
He then defines the highest pleasure as the absence of pain and trouble, which is a different thing. A stone has that.
If pleasure were the highest good, the wisest people would be at the buffet, and he ate bread and water.
Epicurus said pleasure is the goal and then banned everything fun. It's hedonism for people who go to bed at nine.
> Death is nothing to us
Death can be bad for the one who dies by depriving them of the life they'd have had, even though they aren't there to feel it.
If death were nothing to us, nobody would have needed the argument. He wrote it down four separate ways.
"Where death is, I am not." Sure. That's the complaint.
> The gods exist but don't care
Gods that do nothing explain nothing. Keeping them looks like a concession to the neighbors more than a conclusion.
Gods who don't answer, don't act and don't notice are called "no gods." He was an atheist with a lawyer.
His gods are too relaxed to do anything, which is also his ethics.
> Live unnoticed; stay out of politics
Withdrawal is only safe if others keep the city running and defended. As general advice it depends on most people ignoring it.
The Garden had walls, a deed and a legal system protecting both. Somebody was doing the politics.
"Live unnoticed," he wrote, in 300 books, with his name on them.

## Diogenes & the Cynics | Ancients
> Live by nature, not convention
Humans are conventional by nature. Language, cooperation and shared rules are how the animal lives, not an add-on to strip away.
Nature gave people language, and language is a convention. So he used a convention to say that, in a city, to an audience.
Diogenes lived in a jar in the middle of Athens. Rejecting society works best with foot traffic.
> Shamelessness exposes hypocrisy
Shocking people shows that they have norms, which nobody denied. It doesn't show the norms are baseless.
Doing something rude in public proves one thing, which is that it's rude. The audience already knew.
The Cynics were performance artists who got mistaken for philosophers because nobody had invented the grant yet.
> Needing nothing is freedom
Independence bought by wanting almost nothing gives up most of what freedom is for. And he lived on what others gave him.
He needed nothing except the food people handed him, the city he slept in, and someone to watch.
He told Alexander to get out of his sun. Alexander conquered the known world. Diogenes kept the sun.
> I am a citizen of the world
Rights and duties come from actual institutions. Citizenship of everywhere was, in his case, a way of owing nothing to anywhere.
Citizens pay taxes and serve on juries. He was a tourist of the world.
"Citizen of the world" is what someone says when their own city exiled them for a currency scandal.

## The Sophists | Ancients
> Man is the measure of all things
If each person's judgment is true for them, so is the judgment that Protagoras is wrong. Plato pointed that out.
If man is the measure, I measure that he isn't. That's now true, by his rule, and the lesson is over.
"Man is the measure of all things," and the measurement cost fifty drachmas.
> Justice is the advantage of the stronger
The strong can be mistaken about their own advantage, and then obeying them isn't to their advantage. Thrasymachus has to pick one.
The stronger die too, and the laws are still there afterwards. So the laws are stronger, and they say he's wrong.
Thrasymachus lost the argument in book one and sulked for nine more.
> Virtue can be taught, for a fee
If it could be taught reliably, the sons of good men would be good, and the dialogues keep noticing that they often aren't.
Anything that can be taught for a fee has reviews. The reviews were written by Plato.
The Sophists were the first consultants. Confident, expensive, and gone before the results came in.
> Every question has two sides
That two cases can be argued doesn't make them equally good. Skill at making the weaker argument win is skill at misleading.
Does every question have two sides? If yes, then also no.
Debate club is the Sophists' surviving institution, and it shows.

## Democritus & atomism | Ancients
> Only atoms and void exist
It was a lucky guess with no evidence behind it, and his atoms, solid and uncuttable, aren't what physics found.
The word means "uncuttable," and they cut it in 1938. Wrong in the name.
Democritus guessed, and physics spent two thousand years making it look like a prediction.
> Sweet, hot and color exist only by convention
If the senses mislead about all that, the atomist has undercut the evidence for atoms. Democritus has the senses say exactly this back to him.
Sugar is sweet in every country. A convention that nobody agreed to and everyone follows is called a fact.
Color isn't real, he explained, in a world he could only find his way around by color.
> Everything happens by necessity
If thoughts are just atoms falling as they must, there's no reason to trust that they track the truth, including that thought.
Epicurus took the same atoms and added a random swerve because nobody could live with it. Even the atomists left.
They called him the laughing philosopher. If it's all atoms bumping, the laughing was involuntary.
> The goal of life is cheerfulness
It's amiable advice with little argument behind it, and it doesn't follow from atoms and void in any way he shows.
Cheerfulness is some atoms. Telling atoms to be a different arrangement is not ethics, it's yelling at gravel.
His ethics is "be in a good mood." Thanks.

## Eternalism | Metaphysics
> Past, present and future are equally real
The view has trouble with why experience seems to move and why we care differently about what's coming than what's over.
If the future were already real, somebody could go look. Nobody has ever brought anything back, not even a newspaper.
The past is real, the future is real, and somehow the rent is only ever due now.
> Relativity proves the block universe
Relativity removes a universal "now." It doesn't force the block, and physicists and philosophers still defend other readings.
Einstein also said God doesn't play dice. He was wrong about his own field, so his metaphysics isn't a tiebreaker.
"Physics says so" is carrying the whole view, and physics didn't say so.
> The flow of time is an illusion
An illusion is itself something that happens in time. The seeming to flow needs explaining, and "illusion" just renames it.
An illusion takes time to have. So time passes while people are being fooled about whether time passes.
Time doesn't pass, according to people who are late.
> You always exist at the times you exist
That's true on every view of time, so it's no comfort. The loss in dying is the future one doesn't get.
"He still exists, in 1987" has never once helped at a funeral.
Eternalism is "he's in a better place," where the place is last year.

## Why there's something | Metaphysics
> It's the deepest question there is
The question may be ill-formed. "Nothing" isn't a state that could have obtained and needed preventing, so there may be nothing to explain.
There's something because if there weren't, nobody would be asking. That's the answer. It took one line.
"Why is there something rather than nothing" has produced nothing. It's the question answering itself.
> Physics shows a universe can come from nothing
The "nothing" in those accounts is a quantum vacuum with laws and fields, which is something. Critics pointed this out at once.
The physicist's nothing has energy, laws and a budget. That's the kind of nothing people have in their bank account before payday.
A whole book called A Universe from Nothing, and the nothing turned out to be stuff.
> A necessary being explains it
A being whose existence is necessary needs its necessity explained, and it's unclear why the universe couldn't have that status as easily.
"It exists because it has to" works for the universe too, and the universe has the advantage of being here.
The answer to "why is there anything" is a thing. That's the question again, with a beard.
> It's just a brute fact
Brute facts are where explanation stops, and stopping at the largest fact there is looks like giving up exactly where it matters.
"It just is" is what every explanation was invented to avoid saying.
Brute fact is "because I said so," from nobody.

## Many-worlds | Metaphysics
> The wavefunction never collapses; every branch is real
The branches can't be observed even in principle, so the extra worlds do no work that could ever be checked against the alternatives.
If the universe copied itself every time an atom did anything, the energy bill would be noticeable.
Many-worlds says there's a version of you who understood it.
> It's the simplest reading of the equations
It's simple in postulates and enormous in ontology, and it needs extra work to say what a "world" is and why branches look classical.
Occam's razor says don't multiply entities. This multiplies universes, every instant, forever. It's the least simple thing ever proposed.
"Simplest interpretation," they say, pointing at infinity universes.
> Probability comes from uncertainty about which branch you're in
If every outcome happens, it's unclear what "70% likely" means. Deriving the Born rule from rational betting is contested, to put it mildly.
If everything happens, the odds of everything are 100%. So the theory predicts every result, which is the same as predicting none.
In some branch this argument was convincing.
> It just takes quantum mechanics seriously
Every interpretation takes the formalism seriously. They differ on what it describes, and "seriously" doesn't pick one.
Taking the math seriously is how people end up thinking negative numbers of apples exist.
Many-worlds is fan fiction for physicists. What if I'd asked her out. It's in the wavefunction.

## Mathematical platonism | Metaphysics
> Numbers exist independently of us
If numbers are outside space and time and cause nothing, it's a mystery how we could know anything about them.
Where? It's a fair question about anything that exists. Things that exist are somewhere.
Platonists believe in an invisible realm of perfect beings that can't be seen or touched, and get annoyed at religion.
> Mathematics is discovered, not invented
The feeling of discovery is equally expected if we're exploring the consequences of rules we laid down, as in chess.
Different cultures invented different number systems. Nobody discovers something in base ten.
Math is discovered, which is why it comes with so many arbitrary conventions about notation.
> Science can't do without numbers, so they're real
Being indispensable to a theory may show usefulness, not existence. Some argue physics can in principle be done without quantifying over numbers.
Science can't do without frictionless planes either. Go find one.
"We need it, so it exists" is also how people talk about their third coffee.
> We grasp mathematical truths by intuition
A faculty of mathematical perception with no known mechanism is a label for the problem. And intuitions led to contradictions in naive set theory.
Intuition says a line has more points than a shorter line. It doesn't. Intuition failed the first quiz.
Mathematical intuition is a sixth sense that only works for people who already have the degree.

## Nominalism | Metaphysics
> Only particular things exist
Then what two red things share needs explaining without redness. Resemblance is the usual answer, and resemblance looks like a universal.
If only particulars exist, the word "particular" doesn't apply to more than one of them. He needs a new word for each.
Nominalism: there's no such thing as a kind of thing, including this kind of view.
> Universals are just names
Things were alike before anyone named them. The name follows the likeness and can't be what makes it.
Dogs were all dogs before English. Other dogs can tell.
"It's only a word" has never ended an argument and this is the oldest one.
> Mathematics is a useful fiction
A fiction this useful, that predicts the unobserved to twelve decimals, is hard to tell from a truth. The usefulness is the thing needing explanation.
Fiction doesn't keep planes up. Nobody boards a flight designed with Hamlet.
Numbers aren't real, says the man counting his change.
> Occam's razor cuts abstract objects
Parsimony counts only if the leaner theory explains as much. Paraphrasing away every reference to numbers and properties tends to cost more than it saves.
Occam's razor is an abstract object. He's sawing the branch.
Ockham was a friar. He kept one invisible entity, the big one.

## Mereological nihilism | Metaphysics
> There are no tables, only particles arranged tablewise
"Arranged tablewise" seems to presuppose the concept of a table. And if particles themselves have parts, the view may leave nothing at all.
I put my coffee on it. The coffee is not on the floor. The table has more evidence than most of physics.
There are no tables, he typed, at a desk.
> Composition never occurs
Then there are no people either, including the one holding the view. Exceptions for living things look like special pleading.
Then there's no philosopher, no paper and no journal. The view has been published by nothing in nothing.
If nothing is made of parts, neither is the argument, and it needs all three of its premises.
> Ordinary talk is loosely true
A theory that says nearly every ordinary statement is strictly false needs the "loosely true" story to do all the work, and it rarely gets spelled out.
"Loosely true" is "true." He's charging extra for an adverb.
"There's a table" is false but fine to say. So is everything else he says.
> It solves the Ship of Theseus
It removes the puzzle by removing the ship, which is like curing a headache by denying there's a head.
It solves it the way burning the boat solves it.
Asked which ship is the original, the nihilist says "what ship," and thinks that's a move.

## Functionalism | Mind
> Mental states are defined by what they do
Block's objection: organize the population of China to mirror a brain's functions and, on this view, the nation has a mind. Most find that absurd.
A fake Rolex does everything a Rolex does. Ask the pawn shop if that makes it one.
Functionalism: if it walks like a mind and talks like a mind, stop asking.
> The same mind could run on different hardware
Being realizable in many materials is an assumption about minds, not a finding. Digestion and photosynthesis are functions too, and they need the chemistry.
The same recipe in a different oven gives a different cake. Bakers know this. Philosophers don't bake.
The mind is software, according to people who have never had a hangover.
> The mind is to the brain as software is to hardware
Brains have no clean split between program and machine. The wiring is the memory and the chemistry is the computation.
Software has an author, a version number and a license. So who shipped this, and where do the bug reports go?
The software analogy replaced the telephone exchange analogy, which replaced the steam engine one.
> Organized the right way, anything would have a mind
The claim leaves out experience. A system could have every functional role filled, it seems, with nothing it's like to be it.
Then a big enough company has a mind. Anyone who's worked at one can rule that out.
Functionalists think a billion people with walkie-talkies could be in pain.

## Illusionism & Dennett | Mind
> Consciousness is a kind of user illusion
An illusion is an experience of something that isn't so. To be under an illusion there has to be experience, which was the thing to be explained.
For it to seem like anything, something has to be doing the seeming. That's consciousness. He walked past it to get to the argument.
Dennett's book is called Consciousness Explained. Critics have called it Consciousness Explained Away for thirty years and it still fits.
> There's no Cartesian theater in the brain
Almost nobody holds that there's a literal place where it all comes together. Knocking it down leaves the question of experience where it was.
There's no little man in the head, fine. Nobody said little man. He beat a puppet.
He spent fifty years refuting a view nobody held and called everyone who disagreed a secret holder of it.
> Qualia don't exist
Denying the one thing each person seems most directly acquainted with needs overwhelming argument, and "you only think you have it" isn't one.
Pain hurts. That's the datum. A theory that says the hurt isn't there has checked the theory and not the pain.
If there are no qualia, anesthesiologists are paid for nothing.
> Explain the reports and you've explained consciousness
That treats what people say about experience as the whole phenomenon. The question was about what the reports are reports of.
A thermometer reports the temperature. Explaining the thermometer doesn't make the room warm.
"You just think you're conscious" is the only theory that insults the evidence.

## The Chinese room | Mind
> Syntax isn't enough for semantics
The premise is the conclusion in other words. Whether the right processing could amount to understanding is exactly what's being asked.
Brains are neurons following chemical rules. That's syntax. So by the argument nobody understands anything, including the argument.
Searle proved computers can't think using a thought experiment, which is a computation he ran in his head.
> The man in the room doesn't understand Chinese
The systems reply: he's one part. A single neuron doesn't understand English either, and nobody concludes that people don't.
The man would learn Chinese in about a year. People pick up languages. Leave him in there.
The room would need billions of years per sentence. It's a thought experiment where the thought never finishes.
> Simulation isn't duplication
That's true of storms and digestion. Whether minds are like storms or like calculation is the open question, and a simulated calculation is a calculation.
A simulated calculator calculates. A simulated chess player wins. The line gets fuzzy fast when the product is information.
A simulated argument is still an argument, which is bad news for this one.
> Brains cause minds by their biology
"Causal powers of the brain" names whatever makes the difference without saying what it is or why silicon couldn't have it.
Biology is chemistry, chemistry is physics, and physics is math. He drew the line at the one level he liked.
Searle's answer to what brains have that computers don't is "the right stuff," and he never said what stuff.

## Integrated information theory | Mind
> Consciousness is integrated information
The theory implies that simple grids of logic gates, wired the right way, are more conscious than any computer. Even sympathetic critics take that as a refutation.
Then a well-connected spreadsheet beats a jellyfish. Nobody believes that, including the spreadsheet.
A hundred-odd researchers signed a letter calling it pseudoscience. That's the peer review.
> It starts from the self-evident axioms of experience
The axioms aren't self-evident to others in the field, and the step from them to the mathematics is not a derivation anyone else can reproduce.
"Self-evident" is what people call the step they couldn't prove.
The axioms are obvious, the math is unreadable, and the conclusion is that a photodiode has feelings.
> A digital computer isn't conscious, whatever it runs
That makes consciousness depend on physical wiring, so two systems behaving identically could differ completely in experience, with no possible test.
So the same program on different chips is a mind or a rock, and nothing anyone can measure tells which. Convenient.
It's the only theory where the answer to "is it conscious" is "what brand is the motherboard."
> It makes testable predictions
Phi can't be computed for any real brain, since the calculation explodes with size. The tests so far check loose proxies that other theories also predict.
A number nobody can compute isn't a prediction. It's a mood with a Greek letter.
Phi has never been measured for anything bigger than a few logic gates. The theory of consciousness has been tested on a doorbell.

## Extended mind | Mind
> Otto's notebook is part of his mind
A notebook's contents are consulted and interpreted, while beliefs aren't looked up. That difference may be what makes something mental.
Then stealing the notebook is brain surgery, and the stationery store sells organs.
If the notebook is part of his mind, losing it on the bus is a head injury.
> Cognition doesn't stop at the skull
That thinking relies on tools doesn't show the tools think. Causal dependence on something isn't the same as being made of it.
Digestion depends on the fridge. The fridge isn't part of the stomach.
The extended mind is the discovery that people write things down.
> Your phone is part of you
If any reliably used resource counts, minds spread without limit, into libraries and other people. The view needs a boundary it can't easily draw.
Then the phone company owns part of everyone's mind and can raise the rent on it. Which, fine, that one's true.
"My phone is part of my mind" is a philosophy of screen time written by people defending their own.
> If it'd count inside the head, it counts outside
Nothing inside the head works like a notebook, so the parity test compares the notebook to an imaginary inner process invented for the purpose.
If a liver did it outside the body, it'd be a bottle of bleach. Parity isn't a principle, it's a pun.
The whole theory is one example about a man named Otto, from 1998.

## Behaviorism | Mind
> Psychology should study behavior, not inner states
Chomsky's review of Skinner showed that language can't be explained by reinforcement history without smuggling the inner states back in.
People behave differently depending on what they think. So the thing that explains behavior is the thing he ruled out.
Two behaviorists meet. "You're fine. How am I?"
> Mental talk is just talk about dispositions
A perfect actor has the pain behavior without pain, and a stoic has the pain without the behavior. The disposition and the state come apart.
A man holding still and saying nothing has a headache. Find the behavior.
Behaviorism is the view that nobody has ever had a secret.
> Behavior is shaped by reinforcement
Learning has innate limits and biases, and animals form expectations and maps without reward. The lab results stopped fitting decades ago.
Children say sentences nobody ever rewarded them for, mostly at the worst possible moment.
Skinner taught pigeons to play ping-pong, and concluded things about people.
> The inner mind is a ghost in the machine
Rejecting a ghostly substance doesn't require rejecting inner states. Cognitive science kept the inner processing and dropped the ghost.
There's no ghost in the machine, and also no dream last night, apparently, since nobody saw it.
Behaviorism was abandoned, and by its own theory that's because it stopped being rewarded.

## Eliminative materialism | Mind
> Beliefs and desires don't exist
The view is hard to state without them. Asserting it, arguing for it and expecting it to persuade all look like things done with beliefs.
He believes there are no beliefs. That's it. That's the reply.
Eliminativists want you to believe there are no beliefs, and they really want it.
> Folk psychology is a failed theory
It predicts human behavior from moment to moment better than any science we have. That's an odd record for a failed theory.
Folk psychology gets a hundred people to the same restaurant at eight. Neuroscience can't yet predict a sneeze.
"Folk psychology has made no progress in 2,000 years." Neither has the wheel. It works.
> Neuroscience will replace it
New sciences usually refine everyday categories or explain how they're implemented. Wholesale replacement, as with witches, is the rare case.
Chemistry didn't eliminate water. It said what water is. He's betting on the one outcome that almost never happens.
Any day now we'll say "my C-fibers are firing" at the dentist. They've been saying any day now since 1981.
> The view isn't self-refuting
The standard defense says the charge assumes the old framework. But until the replacement vocabulary exists, there's no way to say what the theory asserts.
"That objection only works if assertion exists." And then he asserts that.
It's a philosophy that asks to be taken on faith by people it says have no faith.

## The Gettier problem | Knowledge
> Justified true belief isn't knowledge
The cases depend on intuitions that vary more than assumed, and they may show only that "knows" is loose at the edges, like most words.
A man looks at a stopped clock at the right moment and gets the time right. He knows what time it is. He's on time. Next.
Gettier wrote three pages in 1963 and never needed to publish again. That's the real lesson of the paper.
> Knowledge needs a fourth condition
Sixty years of candidates each fell to a new counterexample. That pattern suggests the project of analysis, not the latest condition, is at fault.
They've added a condition every few years since 1963. At this point knowledge is justified true belief plus a terms of service.
Every proposed fix got its own counterexample, usually involving a barn.
> Knowledge can't be analyzed at all
Declaring knowledge primitive ends the search by fiat and leaves the cases that started it, where belief and knowledge seem to differ by luck, undescribed.
"It can't be broken down" is what people say about a jar they couldn't open.
After fifty years of failed definitions the field announced that not defining it was the answer.
> The cases show something deep about knowledge
They may show something shallow: that luck sometimes makes a well-founded belief true. Nobody outside philosophy finds that surprising.
Nobody has ever lost money, a job or an argument over a Gettier case. Deep things have consequences.
The Gettier problem is the discovery that people are sometimes right by accident.

## Relativism | Knowledge
> Truth is relative to a framework
Then that claim is true only relative to a framework, and people in another framework can truly reject it.
Is that true for everyone, or just for you? Either answer ends it.
Relativism is true for the people who say it and false for everyone they're saying it to.
> There's no view from nowhere
That every view is from somewhere doesn't show all views are equally good. Some standpoints see more, and can show it.
A map is drawn from somewhere and still gets people to the airport.
"There's no neutral standpoint," announced from one.
> Different cultures have different truths
Cultures disagree about what's true. That's a fact about beliefs, and it's compatible with some of them being wrong.
Different cultures believed different things about what causes disease. One of those beliefs came with penicillin.
Every culture has its own truth, and they all use the same airplane.
> Relativism promotes tolerance
If tolerance is good only relative to a framework, the intolerant are right in theirs. The argument for tolerance needs it to be more than relatively good.
If nobody's wrong, the intolerant aren't wrong either. He just handed them a permission slip.
Relativists are very sure, across all cultures, that nobody should be very sure.

## Quine | Knowledge
> There's no line between analytic and synthetic
Grice and Strawson replied that a distinction people apply consistently to new cases exists, even if it resists a non-circular definition.
"All bachelors are unmarried" and "it's raining." If those look like the same kind of sentence, the problem isn't the distinction.
Quine erased the difference between definitions and facts, and dictionaries kept selling.
> Epistemology should be a chapter of psychology
Describing how people form beliefs doesn't say which beliefs they ought to hold. The normative question is the one epistemology was asking.
A study of how people actually do arithmetic would conclude that seven times eight is whatever.
Quine handed philosophy's job to psychology, and psychology didn't want it.
> To be is to be the value of a variable
The test says what a theory is committed to, not what exists. It hands the question of what's real to whoever writes the theory down.
So things exist once they show up in a logic formula. The universe was waiting on notation.
His test for existence is whether a logician needs it. Sets made it. Meaning didn't.
> Translation is indeterminate
In practice translators converge, and constraints from grammar, psychology and shared biology rule out the deviant manuals he imagines.
A native says "gavagai" and points at a rabbit. It means rabbit. Field linguists do this before lunch.
"Gavagai" might mean undetached rabbit parts, and "Quine" might mean a man who never met a translator.

## Scientific realism | Science
> Our best theories are approximately true
Many past theories were successful and are now judged false at the core, like caloric and the ether. Induction on that record isn't encouraging.
Every generation's best theory turned out wrong. This generation's is the first one in history that's right. What are the odds.
Realists believe the current textbook, which is what realists believed about the last one.
> Otherwise the success of science is a miracle
Success might be explained by selection: theories that fail are discarded, so the survivors predict well, true or not.
Ptolemy predicted eclipses for fourteen centuries. If success means truth, the sun went round the earth until 1543.
"It works, so it's true" would have made a realist about epicycles.
> Electrons are real
What survives theory change is often the equations, not the picture of what the entities are. The electron of 1900 and today's share little but the name.
The electron has been a ball, a wave, a cloud and a vibration in a field. It's real, they just can't say what.
Electrons are definitely real, and are also definitely not anything anyone can picture.
> Science gets closer to the truth over time
"Closer" needs a measure of distance from a truth we don't have independent access to, and attempts to define it formally ran into trouble.
Closer to what? Nobody's seen the answer key. He's grading the test against the test.
Science converges on the truth the way a hiker converges on the horizon.

## Occam's razor | Science
> The simplest explanation is usually right
Simplicity depends on the language a theory is stated in, and there's no agreed measure. The history of science is full of the more complicated answer winning.
The simplest explanation for lightning was a god throwing it. One cause, no math.
The simplest explanation is usually that the person citing Occam's razor wants to stop talking.
> Don't multiply entities beyond necessity
"Necessity" does all the work. Whether an entity is needed is the question in dispute, so the rule can't settle it.
One element would be simpler than 118. Chemistry multiplied them anyway, because they were there.
Ockham never wrote the famous version. The razor is itself an unnecessary entity.
> Simplicity is a guide to truth
There's no reason the world must be simple. Simpler theories are easier to use and test, which is a practical virtue and may be nothing more.
The universe has dark matter, eleven kinds of quark behavior and the platypus. It was not consulted on simplicity.
"Simple" means "the one I already believed."
> It rules out God, souls and conspiracies
The razor ranks rival explanations of the same data. It doesn't say what exists, and both sides of each of those disputes claim the simpler account.
A theist says one God is simpler than a trillion brute facts. Same razor. It cuts whichever way it's held.
Occam's razor has ended zero arguments, because both people are holding one.

## Frege | Language & Logic
> Sense and reference are distinct
What a "sense" is has never been made clear, and direct reference theorists argue that names contribute only their bearers.
"The morning star is the evening star." It's Venus. It was always Venus. He built a theory on not knowing that.
Frege discovered that the same thing can have two names.
> Arithmetic is logic
Russell's paradox showed his system inconsistent, and repaired versions need axioms, like the existence of infinitely many things, that aren't plausibly logic.
Logic says if P then P. Arithmetic says there are infinitely many primes. One of those is about something.
Frege spent twenty years reducing arithmetic to logic, and Russell broke it with a one-page letter.
> Meaning is objective, not psychological
Putting meanings in a third realm, neither mental nor physical, raises the question of how minds in this one ever get hold of them.
Meanings are in a realm outside space, time and minds, and somehow every toddler finds it by age two.
He threw psychology out of logic, and logic has been explaining nothing about how people think ever since.
> He founded modern logic
He did, in a notation so unwieldy nobody used it. The logic the world learned came through Peano and Russell.
A founder whose notation nobody could read is a founder nobody followed. Peano did the part that got used.
His notation was two-dimensional and the printers hated it. So did everyone else.

## Russell | Language & Logic
> "The present king of France is bald" is false, not meaningless
Strawson: the sentence doesn't assert that there's a king, it presupposes it, and when the presupposition fails the question of truth doesn't arise.
There's no king of France. So it's not true and not false, it's a mistake. Anyone asked on the street says "France doesn't have a king."
Russell's great contribution was a sentence about a king who doesn't exist. It's still his most cited work.
> Mathematics reduces to logic
Principia needed axioms of infinity and reducibility that nobody thought were logical truths, and then Gödel showed no such system could be complete.
It takes several hundred pages to reach 1+1=2. If the foundation is that much harder than the building, it's not the foundation.
Principia Mathematica proves 1+1=2 a few hundred pages in, with the comment that this is occasionally useful.
> The theory of types solves the paradoxes
It blocks them by forbidding the sentences, which also forbids harmless ones, and the hierarchy can't be stated without violating itself.
He fixed the paradox by making it illegal to say. That's not a solution, that's a noise ordinance.
Russell found the paradox, and his fix was that nobody's allowed to talk about it.
> Philosophy should be done with scientific method
His own views changed with nearly every book, which doesn't suggest a method converging on results.
Science settles things. He held about six philosophies in sixty years and was certain during each.
Russell changed his mind so often that Broad said he produced a new system every few years. He was right about one of them, maybe.

## Kripke | Language & Logic
> Names are rigid designators
Descriptivists reply that names can be tied to descriptions that are themselves rigid, such as "the actual so-and-so."
"Aristotle" refers to Aristotle in every possible world. Yes. That's what a name is. That was the lecture.
Kripke's discovery was that a name is the name of the thing it names.
> Water is necessarily H2O, though we found that out
The necessity depends on how "water" was fixed. If it meant "the clear drinkable stuff," other worlds would have water that isn't H2O.
If it was necessary, nobody would have needed a lab. Necessary things are known from a chair. That's the definition.
Water is H2O in every possible world. It took a philosopher to add "possible world" to a chemistry fact.
> Reference is fixed by a causal chain back to a baptism
Reference can shift: "Madagascar" once named part of the mainland. A chain back to the first use gives the wrong answer there.
Nobody baptized "Santa Claus" by pointing at him. The chain goes back to nothing, and every kid still knows who's meant.
Naming and Necessity is a transcript. He gave it without notes and never wrote the book. That's the level of effort.
> Nothing fixes which rule you're following
If no fact fixes what anyone means, the argument's own words mean nothing in particular. Most readers take that as a reason to reject a premise.
I meant plus. I know because I was there. He can ask.
Kripke proved nobody means anything by "plus," and then everyone kept adding.

## Chomsky | Language & Logic
> Language rests on an innate universal grammar
Decades of proposed universals have shrunk under cross-linguistic evidence, and what remains is thin enough that critics ask what the claim now is.
If grammar was built in, there'd be one language. There are seven thousand, and they don't agree about where the verb goes.
Universal grammar has been revised until the only universal left is that Chomsky was right.
> Children hear too little to learn it from experience
Children hear millions of sentences, and statistical learners get much further from that input than the argument assumed in the 1960s.
Kids hear about thirty million words by age four. That's not a poverty of anything.
The poverty of the stimulus was a claim about how much data kids get, made before anyone counted.
> Language evolved for thought, not communication
The claim that language appeared by a single mutation in one individual, for inner use, has almost no support among people who study evolution.
A language used by one person, silently, isn't a language. It's a guy thinking.
His theory is that the first speaker had nobody to talk to. That's a lecture, not an origin.
> Language models tell us nothing about language
Systems that learn fluent grammar from text alone are at least evidence about what can be learned without built-in rules, which was his central claim.
It learned English from reading. He said that was impossible in 1957, and his answer now is that it doesn't count.
A machine learned grammar from examples and he said it wasn't real grammar. He's been grading the same paper since Eisenhower.

## Sapir–Whorf | Language & Logic

The measured effects are small and local, such as slightly faster color discrimination across a named boundary. The strong version has no support.

Linguistic relativity is real and worth about forty milliseconds.

Translation works, and people learn second languages without reporting a change of universe.

Different languages, different worlds, same traffic.

## The liar paradox | Language & Logic
> Truth needs a hierarchy of languages
Ordinary language has one truth predicate that applies to its own sentences. A hierarchy describes a different, tidier language, not ours.
English doesn't have levels. People say "that's true" about sentences containing "true" all day and nothing explodes.
Tarski solved the liar by moving to a language nobody speaks.

If one contradiction can be true, the usual reason for ever rejecting a theory, that it contradicts itself, is gone.
If contradictions can be true, then they also can't. He has to accept that. Those are his rules.
Dialetheism is true. Also false. Its defenders are fine with this, and also not.

Then "this sentence is not true" comes back: if it's neither, it's not true, which is what it says, so it's true.
"This sentence is not true." Neither? Then it's not true. Then it's right. He moved the problem one inch.

It may reveal only that some grammatical sentences fail to say anything, the way "the largest number" fails to pick anything out.

The liar paradox is 2,400 years old and has produced no lies.

## Vagueness | Language & Logic
> There's no sharp line between a heap and a non-heap
If there's no line, then one grain is a heap or a million grains isn't. Logic forces a line somewhere, however unwelcome.
One grain isn't a heap. Add one, still not. So nothing's ever a heap, and yet there's one in the yard. The argument lost to a pile of sand.
The sorites paradox is the discovery that "pile" isn't a unit.
> There is a sharp line; we just can't know where
It's hard to believe that usage fixes an exact grain at which sand becomes a heap, with nothing about how we talk determining which.
So there's an exact hair that makes a man bald and nobody can ever find it. That's not a theory, it's a ghost story.
Epistemicism says there's a precise minute when someone becomes old, and it's a secret.
> Truth comes in degrees
Degrees just move the problem: there has to be a sharp line where "fully true" ends, and nobody believes in that one either.
"It's 0.6 true that he's tall." Nobody has ever said that. A theory of language should include one thing a person has said.
Fuzzy logic: for when someone wants to be wrong to two decimal places.

Clouds and mountains seem to lack sharp borders whatever we call them. It's unclear that tidying the language would tidy them.
Where does a mountain stop? That's not a word problem. Go stand on it.
"It's just semantics" has been said about every problem by someone who then couldn't solve it.
