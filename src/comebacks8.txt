## Deontology
> Some acts
< Most deontologists hold thresholds: the constraint yields at catastrophe. A weight that holds against ordinary gains and gives only at the extreme is still a different thing from one more cost.
< One lie to save a city is the threshold case, and nearly every deontologist allows it. Try arguing with the view its defenders hold.
< It's caring about what you do to people, as against what merely happens near you. Those were always different.

< If you may violate one to save five, nobody has a right. Everyone has a place in a calculation that happens to be going their way.

> Intentions matter

> The reckless driver

> Who you are
< Too much is exactly the worry Nagel raises: follow the control principle through and the agent shrinks to nothing. He offers it as the problem.
< That's the conclusion, and it's Nagel's, stated as a paradox. You've reached the end of the essay.

> Kant was wrong

## Human rights
> Rights belong
< They can be grounded in what any person needs in order to act at all: freedom and well-being. An agent can't coherently claim those for himself and deny them to others.
< Nobody's found the number seven in a body either.
< Self-evident things often take a catastrophe before anyone writes them down.
> They're universal
< The drafting committee included a Chinese Confucian and a Lebanese Arab, and delegates from four continents signed. People everywhere have since claimed them against their own governments.
< Gunpowder showed up in one place too. Where an idea started doesn't limit where it's true.
< A committee from China, Lebanon, Chile, France and the rest. That's what universal looks like in a hotel.
> Rights are trumps
< Rights limit which reasons a government may act on. When two conflict, the weighing is between rights, and "it would be better on the whole" stays excluded.
< Two trumps get compared with each other. The common good still doesn't get to play a low card over either.
< The core list fits on a page. Inflation at the edges doesn't cancel the page.
> A right exists
< People are imprisoned in the name of rights their state denies, and we don't say they had none. The claim on others comes first, and enforcement is what we owe it.
< A right nobody enforces is what every reform movement started with. Then somebody enforced it.
< And the name of the thing they were owed is how anyone knew it was wrong.

## Retributivism
> Wrongdoers deserve
< Vengeance has no limit and no rule. Retribution answers to proportion, guilt and due process, which is why it protects the innocent where deterrence can't.
< The second one isn't a wrong. That's the claim. A fine isn't theft and a prison term isn't kidnapping.
< Revenge goes after whoever's in reach. This goes after the one who did it, and stops.
> Punishment should fit
< It gives an ordering, and that does real work: murder more than theft, never more than was done. The exact tariff is convention inside those bounds.
< They differ on the number and agree on the ranking. No country gives more for shoplifting than for murder.
< Nobody can say how many dollars a broken arm is either, and courts award damages daily.
> Punishing for deterrence
< Few hold the pure view. The usual one is that desert is required and sets the ceiling, and effects decide what to do within it.
< A prison aimed only at making people safer would hold whoever is statistically risky. Desert is what stops that.
< He says it to show the debt is owed to justice and not to anyone's convenience. It's a thought experiment with a point.
> The guilty must be punished
< Honoring the difference between the guilty and the innocent is a good, and it's one that isn't a benefit to any particular person.
< He said no further good. Justice being done is the good. You stopped reading one clause early.
< Somebody has to, or the victim's wrong is officially nothing.

## Just war theory
> A war can be just
< Demanding standards are the point. The same theory is what lets anyone say a given war was unjust, and that includes the pacifist's best arguments.
< Both sides claim justice in every lawsuit too. That's why there's a standard to hold them to.
< It's the only vocabulary in which a war has ever been condemned by the country fighting it.
> Civilians may never
< The principle adds duties: take real risks to reduce the harm, and stop if it's out of proportion. A commander who merely "didn't aim" hasn't met it.
< It works in the seminar and at the tribunal. People have been convicted on exactly that distinction.
< You also have to be trying not to let it happen. That clause is why it's four conditions long.
> Soldiers on both sides
< Soldiers can rarely know whether their war is just and are compelled to serve. The equality holds the right people responsible: those who decided.
< A nineteen-year-old conscript didn't invade anywhere. His government did.
< It's what stops the winners from shooting every prisoner.
> Force must be proportionate
< There's no formula, and the limit still excludes cases: nobody defends leveling a city to capture one officer. A vague boundary is a boundary.
< That's an argument for independent review. Nobody abolishes accounting because companies fudge.
< It has come out "no" in war rooms, and those are the strikes you never heard about.
